# Project: Ibasho

## Claude's addition — 0. Why this exists

> Drafted by Claude from the creator's own words. She should rewrite it in her voice.

Many people of the creator's generation cannot afford the homes, holidays and lives they imagined. Ibasho is a safe haven where they can live in that imagined world, built first for herself: a place to tell her story and a world built on her beliefs about how people coexist. The affordability point is her observation and remains a hypothesis until sourced. The economy is a long-term question, not a starting feature. Check new features against this section.

## 1. Purpose

Ibasho is a browser-based virtual place designed around belonging, shared experiences and the freedom to shape a world together.

The name is inspired by Japanese **居場所 (ibasho)**: a place where someone feels they belong, can be present and can be themselves.

The project is both:
1. A creative experiment in designing a small digital world.
2. A hands-on learning project for building, shipping and communicating a technology product.

The initial version is for personal exploration. Inviting other people can come later.

## 2. Product vision

Start with one beautiful, welcoming palace or villa. Make it feel like a place rather than a conventional game lobby.

Possible spaces:
- A central hall and comfortable lounge.
- A library or reading room.
- A music room and dance floor.
- A creative studio.
- A courtyard or café-like gathering area.
- A fireplace lounge.
- Gardens, balconies and rooftops.
- A pool or other outdoor spaces if they fit the first environment.

The first release does not need every space. It should prove that one small environment can feel inviting and be enjoyable to explore.

## 3. Experience principles

- A sense of place matters more than a large map.
- People should be able to explore without needing to understand complicated game mechanics.
- Shared experiences should feel natural: sitting together, talking, listening to music or hosting a gathering.
- The environment should have atmosphere: lighting, time of day, weather and ambient sound can evolve over time.
- The world should eventually give inhabitants meaningful agency to create and change things.
- Accessibility, safety, privacy and moderation must be considered before opening the experience to a wider public.

## 4. Product layers

Build these layers in order:

### Layer 1 — World and environment
A 3D environment, camera, lighting, materials, architecture, furniture and navigation.

### Layer 2 — People and shared experiences
Avatars, movement, presence, interactions, chat, shared audio and eventually voice.

### Layer 3 — A living society
Personal spaces, homes, shared resources, gardening or farming, exchange, community rules and a simple economy.

Do not build the society/economy layer before the first two layers are useful and enjoyable.

## 5. MVP: the first small proof

The first milestone is deliberately modest:

- A browser page that loads a small 3D room.
- A camera that can look around the room.
- Basic lighting and a warm visual focal point, such as a fireplace.
- A simple way to run the project locally.
- A README that explains how to run it.

The next milestone adds a controllable avatar and basic movement.

Multiplayer is important to the long-term vision, but it should follow a working single-player prototype. This keeps early debugging manageable and makes it easier to understand the fundamentals.

## 6. Longer-term feature ideas

These are possibilities, not MVP commitments:

- Avatar creation and customisation.
- Walking, sitting and interacting with objects.
- Multiple rooms connected by doors and corridors.
- Private and public gatherings.
- Text chat, then shared voice.
- Shared music or room-based audio.
- Dynamic day/night, weather and ambient environment.
- Voice commands for navigation and simple actions.
- Personal rooms or homes.
- Gardens, farming, crafting, barter or trade.
- Community-created places and activities.

## 7. Non-goals for the first prototype

- A huge open world.
- A complex virtual currency or marketplace.
- Real-money transactions.
- Advanced AI characters.
- Mobile-native apps.
- A polished commercial-grade multiplayer platform.
- Building every planned room before testing the first one.

## 8. Constraints and working assumptions

- Keep the first prototype free or close to zero-cost.
- Use free/openly licensed tools and assets where possible; check each asset's licence before use.
- Avoid paid APIs and subscriptions until a real need is demonstrated.
- Expect that reliable multiplayer hosting, storage, voice, moderation and scale may eventually have costs.
- Keep the code understandable. The project is also intended to teach its creator how the technology works.
- Use AI as a collaborator and tutor, not as a substitute for testing or product decisions.

## 9. How we will judge progress

Early success means:
- The room loads reliably in a browser.
- The scene feels coherent and inviting.
- The user can navigate it without confusion.
- The project can be run locally from documented instructions.
- Each feature is understood well enough to be maintained and changed.

Later success means that other people can enter, find one another and enjoy spending time there—and eventually contribute to shaping the world.

## 10. Open questions

Keep these open until a prototype helps answer them:
- Visual direction: realistic, stylised, whimsical or a mix?
- Camera: third-person, first-person or switchable?
- Avatar style and degree of customisation?
- Which interactions make the first room feel alive?
- What should be private, public or invite-only?
- What moderation and safety tools are needed before inviting others?
- Which hosting and persistence approach is affordable and reliable?
